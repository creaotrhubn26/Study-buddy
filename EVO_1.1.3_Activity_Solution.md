## The method: how to run your own session

### Step 1: Choose a brand or product

Pick something you know, and **write down why you picked it**. Your familiarity is an advantage and a bias at the same time: it helps you read ambiguous posts, and it means you already have a view. Recording that lets a reader weigh your coding decisions.

### Step 2: Conduct social media monitoring

| Decision | Do this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Platforms** | Choose **two**, not one, and record which | Complaint culture differs sharply by platform. Two lets you tell a real theme from one community's norms |
| **Search terms** | Write them down **before** you start | Searching "brand problems" and "brand review" return different populations. Choosing terms after seeing results is selective evaluation |
| **Coding rules** | Agree your tie-breakers before reading | A tally means something only if the same post gets the same code on Monday and on Friday |
| **Capture** | Fill the sheet **as you read**, not afterwards | Coding from memory over-weights the vivid, which is the exact bias this lesson opened with |
| **Scope** | Record every item in the window, **including neutral ones** | Skipping the boring posts inflates both tails, and is the most common way this exercise goes wrong |
| **Stopping** | Stop when the timer stops | A fixed window makes the sample defensible. Stopping when you have enough of what you expected does not |

### Coding frame

| Code | Assign when | Worked edge case |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Satisfaction, praise, or would recommend | "Better than I expected" is positive, even though it implies low expectations |
| **Negative** | Dissatisfaction, a problem, or would not recommend | "Took three weeks to arrive" is negative even if stated calmly |
| **Neutral** | A question, a fact, or a comparison with no evaluation | "Costs more than IKEA" is neutral unless the poster says that is bad |
| **Mixed** | Praise and complaint in one post | Code the **primary** point, note the secondary as a theme. Never count one post twice |

Three rules prevent the common failures:

1. **One post, one code.** Otherwise the total exceeds the number of posts and every percentage is wrong.
2. **Code the experience, not the tone.** Tone-based coding drifts with your mood across 45 minutes.
3. **Record the neutral items.** They are data.

**A self-consistency check worth five minutes:** when you finish, re-code the first ten items without looking at your original codes. If more than one or two differ, your rules were too loose and the tally is softer than it looks. This is the single-coder version of inter-rater reliability, and reporting that you ran it is worth more than a clean number that was never checked.

## Worked example — constructed data, Nordtre AS

**Session:** Nordtre AS · Reddit and Instagram · 45 minutes · search terms fixed in advance: "Nordtre review", "Nordtre desk", "Nordtre levering".

### Step 3: Analyse the feedback

**Sentiment tally**

| Category | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Positive | 29 | 50.0% |
| Negative | 19 | 32.8% |
| Neutral | 10 | 17.2% |
| **Total** | **58** | **100%** |
| **Net sentiment** (positive % − negative %) | | **+17.2** |

Net sentiment uses the same construction as NPS, and it inherits the same weakness: it discards the neutrals entirely.

**Theme frequency**

| Theme | Count | Sentiment | Why it matters |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Timber and build quality | 11 | Positive | The product promise is landing |
| Delivery time | **9** | Negative | Largest single complaint theme |
| Assembly experience | 8 | Positive | A differentiator against flat-pack expectations |
| Sustainability credentials | 6 | Positive | Named as a purchase **reason**, not a nice-to-have |
| Fit and dimensions questions | 6 | Neutral | Product pages are not answering an obvious question |
| Missing hardware | 5 | Negative | Quality control, with a measurable cost in resends |
| Customer service | 4 | Positive | Recovery works when something goes wrong |
| Price versus alternatives | 3 | Negative | Positioning, not a defect |
| Price questions | 3 | Neutral | Low value, free to answer |
| **Checkout problems** | **2** | Negative | **Small count, large meaning — see below** |
| Comparison questions | 1 | Neutral | Consideration-stage interest |
| **Total** | **58** | | Cross-checks against the sentiment total |

**Platform split**

| Platform | Items | Negative items | Negative share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Reddit | 42 | 16 | 38.1% |
| Instagram | 16 | 3 | 18.8% |

Reddit carries twice the negative rate of Instagram. That is a **platform-culture difference**, not a finding about the product — and it is precisely why two platforms were used. A session run on Instagram alone would have reported net sentiment far higher and missed most of the delivery complaints.

### The most important finding is not the most frequent one

Delivery time is mentioned **9 times**. Checkout problems appear **twice**. The checkout theme is still the more actionable finding.

Activity 1.1.2 measured Nordtre's D2C conversion rate at **1.16%, down 12.4% year on year**, while sessions rose 38%. Two posts describing failed card payments are the **qualitative cause** of a quantitative effect that is already costing measurable revenue.

This is the lesson's central claim in one example: **the quantitative half supplies the size, the qualitative half supplies the cause.** Ranking themes by count alone would have buried the finding under delivery complaints.

It is also a warning about the tally itself. A frequency count answers "what do people mention most", which is a different question from "what should we fix first".

### Step 4: Summarise the findings

**What is the general sentiment toward the brand?**
Net sentiment is **+17.2** and half of all captured items are positive, so the reception is favourably tilted — with the heavy caveat below that this describes 58 self-selected posts and not Nordtre's customers.

**What are the common praises?**
Timber and build quality (11), assembly experience (8), and sustainability credentials (6). The third is the most commercially interesting: customers name FSC-certified Norwegian timber as a **reason for buying**, not merely as something they approve of.

**What are the common complaints?**
Delivery time (9) dominates, followed by missing hardware in flat-pack boxes (5). Both are fulfilment problems rather than product problems, which is a useful distinction: the product promise is landing and the operation behind it is not keeping up.

**Are customers asking for anything?**
Two requests recur. **Clearer dimension information on product pages** — six neutral questions ask about fit, which is a question the site should be answering before someone has to ask. And **delivery tracking**, appearing inside the delivery complaints: several posts object less to the wait than to not knowing.

### Recommended actions, in priority order

| Priority | Action | Justified by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Investigate the checkout payment failures | Attaches to a measured 12.4% conversion decline, so the revenue effect is already quantified |
| 2 | Add dimensions and fit guidance to product pages | Six neutral questions are six people who had to ask; many more will have left instead |
| 3 | Publish realistic delivery estimates and add tracking | The complaint is as much about uncertainty as about the wait |
| 4 | Audit flat-pack hardware packing | Five resends have a direct cost and a disproportionate reputational effect |
| 5 | Use the sustainability message more prominently | It is already a stated purchase reason and is underused in the funnel |

## Step 5: Reflection

**What I learned about the brand from customer feedback.**
The clearest thing is that the **product** and the **operation** are being judged separately. Every praise theme is about the object — timber, assembly, sustainability — and every complaint theme is about getting it into the customer's hands: delivery time, missing hardware, checkout. That split would not have been visible in any single number. A satisfaction score would have averaged the two into a middling figure that describes neither.

The second thing is that a **small theme can outrank a large one**. Two checkout posts mattered more than nine delivery posts because the first attaches to a KPI with a measured value and the second, while real, describes a problem already known. Frequency told me what people talk about; the connection to a measured KPI told me what to fix.

**How social media monitoring is valuable to businesses.**
Its genuine strengths are **immediacy** and **unprompted content**. A survey asks the questions you thought to ask; social media surfaces the ones you did not. Nobody would have designed a survey question about card payment failures, because nobody knew the problem existed. It is also fast and effectively free, which makes it viable as a continuous early-warning channel rather than a quarterly exercise.

It is also a **leading indicator**. Complaints appear before churn does, so acting on them is still possible when they are noticed — which is the whole argument for collecting qualitative data at all rather than waiting for the financial numbers to confirm the damage.

**And what it cannot do.**
Social media is the most self-selected source in the lesson. People post when they are delighted or furious; the satisfied middle never appears. So the method is genuinely useful for **detecting a change** and unreliable for **stating a level**. Reporting "50% of customers are positive" from this data would be a straightforward misuse — 50% of *these 58 posts* were positive, and those posts are not a sample of anything.

The right way to use it is as one input among several. Pair it with a method that reaches the middle, such as a post-purchase NPS survey with an open-text follow-up, and reconcile the themes: a complaint appearing in both sources is real, and one appearing in only one tells you something about the source.

## Limitations (state these with any figure above)

| Limitation | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Self-selection** | The satisfied middle is invisible, so no share here estimates a population value |
| **Not a random sample** | The search terms and platforms determined what was found |
| **Single 45-minute window** | One session cannot separate a trend from a bad week. Repeat with identical terms and window |
| **Single coder** | All coding is one person's judgement. Re-coding the first ten blind is the cheapest available check |
| **Visible themes only** | A problem nobody posts about is absent from this data entirely |
| **Constructed dataset** | This particular example is illustrative and observed nothing |

## Appendix: method choices

| Decision | What was chosen | Why | What was rejected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data provenance | A labelled constructed dataset about a fictional company | Firsthand observation cannot be delegated; a labelled example still shows the shape of a complete answer | Inventing posts about a real brand and presenting them as observed — the data-integrity failure this lesson names |
| Platforms | Two | Complaint culture differs sharply; two separates a theme from a platform norm | One — the 38% versus 19% negative split would have been invisible |
| Search terms | Fixed before the session | Terms chosen after seeing results is selective evaluation | Adjusting mid-session to find more of what you expected |
| Coding rules | Written frame with edge cases | Same post, same code, on Monday and Friday | Coding by feel, which drifts across 45 minutes |
| One post, one code | Mixed posts coded on their primary point | Otherwise the total exceeds the post count and every share is wrong | Counting a mixed post twice |
| Neutral items | Captured | Skipping them inflates both tails | Recording only positives and negatives |
| Theme ranking | By meaning, not count | The two checkout posts attach to a quantified revenue effect | Ranking by frequency, which buries the most actionable finding |
| Self-consistency | Re-code the first ten blind | The single-coder version of inter-rater reliability | Reporting a tally that was never checked |
