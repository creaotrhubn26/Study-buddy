## ARP Task 1.3 Workplace overview and Harvard referencing

Task 1.3 asks for a 500-word overview titled 'The Data Analyst in the Workplace', with at least four citations followed by a Bibliography/Reference list in Harvard style. Use Google Scholar to find a few good sources. The brief says at least four citations, not explicitly four distinct sources. Four separate sources may add breadth, but are not a stated minimum. No Harvard institutional variant, word-count inclusion rule, tolerance, file format, font, page count, required software or mandatory figures is specified. Confirm the Harvard variant and whether references and in-text citations count toward 500 words; do not invent a ±10% allowance. The five-paragraph word budget is a Study Buddy suggestion, not an official marking scheme. Cite paraphrases as well as quotations; match each citation to a verified reference and supporting passage. Never fabricate an author, year, title, DOI, quotation, page locator, source-reading claim or completed review. Scholar and citation generators are discovery/formatting aids, not the original evidence. The lesson's John Doe sources are dummy demonstrations and must not be cited as real research. Chicago examples are not the required Harvard style. Starter papers are optional, not an approved Noroff reading list or proof that the learner has read them. Exam Resolver reviews the supplied draft and source notes in English; it cannot certify unread sources, native Word formatting, an official word count or a submission from a written plan. The learner checks the saved file and submits it.

### 1. Decode the overview brief
Record the exact topic: The Data Analyst in the Workplace. Plan a 500-word overview, at least four in-text citations and a following Harvard reference list. Confirm the institution's Harvard variant and what counts toward 500 words. The brief gives no word-count tolerance or required file format.

Check: Distinguish an overview of the workplace role from an essay about citation software. Four citations does not automatically mean four distinct sources.

### 2. Search Google Scholar and screen sources
Start with the linked Scholar search, then refine terms such as data analyst competencies, enterprise data analysis, and knowledge sharing data analysts. Open the original article or author/publisher page, not just a search snippet. Check author, year, title, publication, DOI, relevance and access. Record whether you read the full text or only the abstract. The starter cards below are optional research leads.

Check: A result in Scholar is not proof of peer review or accuracy. Do not claim to have read a paywalled full paper when you only saw its abstract.

### 3. Build a claim-to-source evidence matrix
For each planned claim, write a source ID, your own note, the supporting section or real page locator, and the limits of that evidence. Plan at least four citation placements across the overview. Reusing a relevant source is allowed by the wording; use a sensible range for breadth. Never invent a page number or turn an interview finding into a universal fact about every workplace.

Check: Every source-backed claim can be traced to a passage actually consulted. Blank evidence cells remain unresolved rather than becoming invented support.

### 4. Plan five focused paragraphs
Use this optional 500-word budget: role and purpose 70; data preparation 120; analysis and interpretation 110; communication and responsibility 130; conclusion 70. The total is 500. Explain the role, how data becomes useful evidence, and how findings support decisions. Connect paragraphs rather than listing software. Adapt the budget if the tutor's counting rule includes references or citations differently.

Check: The five-paragraph plan is a scaffold, not a required structure. Avoid promising universal tools, guaranteed business outcomes or causal effects unsupported by your sources.

### 5. Draft and insert Harvard citations
Write in your own words from your evidence notes. Place author-date citations next to the claims they support, for example (Kandel et al., 2012) or Collier and Powell (2024). Cite paraphrases too. For a direct quotation, reproduce the exact wording and add a verified page or other locator required by the chosen Harvard guide. Prefer concise paraphrases in a short overview.

Check: There are at least four meaningful in-text citation placements. A reference list alone is not four citations. Do not copy text and merely change a few words.

### 6. Build and cross-check the reference list
Create an alphabetical list with full details for every cited source, following one agreed Harvard variant. Check authors, publication year, title, journal, volume, issue, page range and DOI against the original publication record. A journal article's whole page range is not the pinpoint page of a quotation. Use Word, Zotero, Mendeley Cite or manual formatting as available, then verify their output.

Check: Every in-text citation has a matching entry, and every reference-list entry is cited. If using a broader bibliography, label consulted-only items according to the tutor's guidance rather than pretending they were cited.

### 7. Review the word count and saved document
Read for focus, evidence and clear English. Apply the tutor's confirmed counting rule; use Word's count on the relevant text and record the result. Check at least four citations, alphabetical Harvard entries, claim support and unresolved placeholders. Save and reopen your work. Prepare Task 1.3 in Exam Resolver to review the draft and notes; make final corrections in your document and submit it yourself.

Check: An AI estimate is not an official Word count or proof that the sources support your claims. The task does not require the conceptual images to be included in your submission.

### Suggested paragraph budget
- Role and purpose: 70 words. Explain why the workplace needs the analyst and what decisions the work can inform.
- Data preparation: 120 words. Explain acquiring, understanding and checking data before analysis; map a citation to actual evidence.
- Analysis and interpretation: 110 words. Explain suitable methods, interpretation and uncertainty; distinguish association from causation.
- Communication and responsibility: 130 words. Connect findings to readers and decisions; discuss limits and appropriate handling of data without inventing legal rules.
- Conclusion: 70 words. Bring together the role's value and the need for evidence, context and clear communication. Introduce no unsupported finding.

### Final checks
- **Topic and length:** The overview addresses the workplace role and meets 500 words under the confirmed counting rule; no invented tolerance.
- **At least four citations:** Count in-text citation placements and check the source/claim link, not just bibliography entries.
- **Harvard reference list:** One Harvard variant, alphabetical entries and complete verified metadata after the overview.
- **Two-way matching:** Each in-text citation matches a reference; reference-list entries are cited in the text.
- **Evidence and final file:** The actual passages were consulted, quotations/locators checked, placeholders removed and saved file reopened.
