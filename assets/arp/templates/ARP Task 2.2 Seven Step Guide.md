## Task boundary

Task 2.2 concerns fictional car dealership Nice Ride. Create an MS Word report showing the most popular brand from the six supplied cars-sold counts: Mercedes 220, Audi 180, Volkswagen 150, Skoda 130, Volvo 100, Tesla 85. Use text formatting including different heading styles, a Column plot (vertical columns), and a short comment following the plot. Mercedes has the highest count, 220; total 865, and its lead over Audi is 40 cars. Most popular means highest recorded sales count within this supplied dataset. These are fictional aggregate counts, not national market share or proof of preference, causation, profitability or future demand. No reporting period, shop-level breakdown, prices, costs or customer-level data are supplied. Do not invent them. The brief specifies no word count, fixed page count, mandatory title page, table of contents, citation style, linked/embedded chart experiment, PowerPoint or imagegen-image submission. Keep the Word report proportionate to the quick-report request. The lesson's chocolate-bar workbook is a separate demonstration, not the car-sales task dataset. The workbook download is a Study Buddy learning aid with a native editable column chart; the Word template contains editable headings and placeholder prose plus a static chart picture, not a linked Excel object. If the source counts change, replace the Word picture and recheck the comment. Study images and written notes are not proof of completed Word work. Review the selected step and draft in English, flag missing evidence or placeholders, and have the learner inspect the actual saved report and submit it themselves.

## 1. Read the Nice Ride brief

Open the original task. The manager wants a quick MS Word report about how many cars of each brand were sold and which brand is most popular in the supplied data. Record the four practical requirements: Word report, heading styles, a Column plot and a short comment after that plot. Use the six task counts, not the separate chocolate-bar lesson example.

Check: The task does not set a word or page count. A long report, PowerPoint, new survey or one-page article review is not required.

## 2. Enter and validate the six brand counts

Use the downloadable CSV or workbook, or type a two-column Brand / Cars sold table in Excel. Check the exact pairs: Mercedes 220, Audi 180, Volkswagen 150, Skoda 130, Volvo 100, Tesla 85. In the supplied workbook these data are B10:C15, with headers in B9:C9. Preserve the original values and keep numerical cells as numbers.

Check: There are six brands and 865 cars in total. Do not include the total or summary cells as another brand in the chart. No individual shops or time periods are specified.

## 3. Identify the leading brand and keep the scope clear

Compare the six counts. Mercedes is highest at 220, followed by Audi at 180, a difference of 40 cars. Tesla is lowest at 85. An optional calculation is Mercedes' share of these six-brand sales: 220 / 865 × 100, approximately 25.4%. Use this only if useful and name the denominator. Counts alone are enough to answer the task.

Check: Highest count is not a majority: 220 is less than half of 865. The data do not explain why a brand sold more, nor whether it earned more profit or was more available in stock.

## 4. Create a vertical column chart

Select the Brand and Cars sold table including headers and all six data rows. Choose a two-dimensional clustered column chart in Excel. In the downloadable workbook the native chart is already linked to B9:C15; inspect its categories and values. Use brands on the horizontal axis and Cars sold on the vertical axis with a zero baseline. Keep all six labels readable and avoid 3D effects or a pie/line/horizontal-bar substitute.

Check: The six columns must match 220, 180, 150, 130, 100 and 85 in the displayed brand order. Check the chart source range; a total of 865 must not become a seventh column.

## 5. Structure the Word report and insert the chart

Open the Word template or create a new Word report. Use a Title style for the document name and Heading 1 for sections such as Introduction, Sales by brand and Conclusion; use Heading 2 only where a real subsection helps. Explain the purpose and fictional dataset, then insert the verified column chart at a readable size. The supplied Word template already contains a static chart picture, with the numerical data available separately in Excel.

Check: Bold text alone is not a semantic heading style. A static chart picture can satisfy the plot requirement; linking or embedding an Excel workbook is not required. If using Word in a browser, insert a verified chart image if native chart tools are unavailable, then inspect the saved DOCX.

## 6. Write the short comment immediately after the plot

State the main pattern using the actual counts, then give one useful comparison and a scoped interpretation. For example, Mercedes leads at 220, Audi follows at 180 and Tesla has 85. Explain that Mercedes is most popular by the supplied recorded sales, while the data do not establish why. Write your own concise paragraph after the chart; do not leave the template prompt or move the only explanation before the plot.

Check: Every numerical statement must match the source and chart. Do not add dates, trends, shop rankings, customer motives, profit or stocking recommendations that these aggregate counts do not support.

## Worked comment to study and adapt

Mercedes recorded the highest sales in the supplied Nice Ride data, with 220 cars, compared with Audi's 180. Tesla had the lowest count at 85. Mercedes is therefore the most popular brand by recorded sales within this dataset. The figures do not establish the reasons for these differences.
